1. CHOKING MECHANISMS AND MACH NUMBER LIMITS
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
While empirical correlations (like Aungier's X parameter) are useful for 
identifying the onset of choking at the geometric throats (impeller and 
vaned diffuser inlet), they are insufficient to capture the full physics 
of the machine. 
During the off-design sweep for Ammonia (NH3), the solver failed to 
converge before the vaned diffuser throat parameter (X_vd) became positive. 
This revealed that the true physical bottleneck of the compressor was 
the Vaned Diffuser Outlet (Station 4), where the absolute Mach number 
(M4) reached 1.0. 
Conclusion: Choking must be defined thermodynamically (M >= 1 at any 
station), not just geometrically at the throats.


2. THE IMPACT OF THE REYNOLDS NUMBER ON COMPRESSOR DESIGN
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
A critical error in early iterations was using the Reynolds number of the 
Vaneless Diffuser (based on the machine's full outer diameter) to calculate 
the friction coefficient (Cf) for the Vaned Diffuser.
Because the machine diameter is massive compared to the narrow channels 
between the diffuser vanes, this artificially inflated the Reynolds number, 
which artificially reduced the friction coefficient. 
Conclusion: To accurately predict internal losses, the characteristic 
length for channel flow must strictly be the local Hydraulic Diameter 
(D_h). Correcting this dropped the predicted design efficiency from ~81% 
down to a physically rigorous 79.3%.


3. DIMENSIONLESS COMPRESSOR MAPS (THE FLOW COEFFICIENT)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
When plotting compressor maps across multiple rotational speeds, using the 
dimensional mass flow rate (kg/s) or the standard flow coefficient 
(Phi = Cm / U2) causes the speed lines to stack vertically or overlap 
unphysically. 
Because Phi normalizes out the rotational speed (U2), it destroys the 
horizontal "spread" characteristic of real compressor maps.
Conclusion: To create a professional, dimensionless compressor map, the 
X-axis must use the Dimensionless Mass Flow Parameter (m_nd), which 
normalizes mass flow using solely the inlet stagnation conditions 
(m_nd = m_dot / (rho_t1 * a_t1 * D2^2)). This perfectly preserves the 
aerodynamic envelope (Surge and Choke lines) across all speeds.


4. VERIFICATION WITH HYDROGEN (H2) AND THE SPEED OF SOUND
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
When the geometry optimized for Ammonia was tested with Hydrogen at the 
same 15,000 RPM, the performance collapsed: the pressure ratio barely 
exceeded 1.09, and the machine never choked, even at maximum flow.
This is fundamentally due to Hydrogen's incredibly low molar mass 
(2 g/mol), which results in a massive gas constant (R) and an extremely 
high speed of sound (a > 1300 m/s). 
Conclusion: At 15,000 RPM, the impeller tip speed (U2 = 460 m/s) is 
supersonic for Ammonia but deeply subsonic (M < 0.35) for Hydrogen. 
The compressor essentially acts as a fan moving an incompressible fluid. 
To compress Hydrogen effectively, the impeller would need to spin at 
catastrophic speeds that exceed the mechanical stress limits of titanium.


5. NEGATIVE EFFICIENCY AND STONEEWALLING
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
At extreme mass flow rates (far to the right on the map), the pressure 
ratio (beta_tt) drops below 1.0 and the isentropic efficiency becomes 
negative. 
Conclusion: This occurs because the aerodynamic losses (friction, 
incidence, and separation) scale with the square of the velocity (V^2). 
Eventually, these internal pressure losses overpower the pressure rise 
generated by the impeller's Euler work. The machine stops acting like a 
compressor and becomes an active restrictor valve. Because the shaft is 
still consuming power (positive actual work) to output a fluid at a lower 
pressure than it entered (negative ideal isentropic work), the mathematical 
result is a negative efficiency.
